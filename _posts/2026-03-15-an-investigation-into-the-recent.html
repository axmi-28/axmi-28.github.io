It’s nighttime in Gaepung County, North Korea. The entire village is silent save for the distant clacking of soldiers patrolling for curfew. Bordering the DMZ from the North, the county harbors a sizable contingent of troops and a handful of laborers and their families.

One of these soldiers, a young man who had just turned 20, was returning to his cramped dorm in the dilapidated shared barracks. He had been enlisted for four years now, serving long hard hours manning observation posts, cleaning toilets and floors, dragging sacks of rice and coal. Outside, distant music and speech began to sound, recognizable as broadcasts of North Korean martial songs praising the Ruler. They were meant to be projected across the DMZ to the South Korean border, but Gaepung was nevertheless assailed by the noise daily. Minutes later, the North Korean broadcastings halted. Even more faintly than the previous broadcastings, cheerful, rhythmic music filled the air soon after. The young man sat up and creaked open the window, straining his ears. It was a South Korean broadcast this time, and the sounds were clearly pop songs.

“Dyn-na-na-na, na-na-na-na-na, na-na-na, life is dynamite

Shining through the city with a little funk and soul

So Ima light it up like dynamite, whoa-oh-oh.”

The young man smiled slightly. He swore he had heard the song before in a previous broadcast, nodding his head to the catchy rhythm. Everything he’d been taught insisted that the South was a puppet of the Americans, a broken society where only a privileged few lived well. Still, as the bright and buoyant melody pulsed through the night air, he softened: surely no nation that sounded like that could be entirely as the broadcasts claimed.

The Rise of Hallyu

Hallyu, a term that translates to “Korean Wave” in Chinese, was first mentioned in a Beijing newspaper in 1999.1 It had attempted to put a name to the sudden popularity of Korean TV dramas in the late 20th century, both in China and surrounding Asian countries including Japan. This initial burst in attention had been unexpected from a nation that still lingered in many Western countries’ minds as a third-world nation, yet fast forward two decades, and the international stage has been flooded in the “Wave.” Everything from the flashy, catchy songs of the BTS boy band, to the wildly popular TV show Squid Game, to even Korean beauty products and procedures, are all right there, smack middle in the public popular spotlight. So how did we get here?

Perhaps the most logical place to begin is 1997, the Asian Financial Crisis and the arrival of the Kim Dae-jung regime.2 His priorities marked a profound shift in government strategy, where culture as an industry became considered as one of the “key driving forces in the national development strategy of Korea.” The government would launch the Hallyu Industrial Support Developmental Plan in 2001,3 boosting the “cultural budget” by 500% and encouraging government-backed music studios to nurture Backstreet Boy-like pop stars infused with a Korean aesthetic and hybridized with Michael Jackson’s dance moves. Seemingly overnight, Korean won began the conversion into catchy choruses, right in time for the internet boom. In 2009, the Korea Creative Content Agency (KOCCA) would be established with the purpose of uniting the government-backed creative industry from coordinating the shiny private facilities where idols were forged, to providing government oversight of “K-Content AI Innovation” projects in 2025 – whatever that might entail. The KOCCA would even launch physical centers in over thirty countries, from Sao Paulo to Madrid, promoting and marketing everything from the latest Squid Game season to an upcoming Blackpink tour.3 Korean presidents themselves explicitly lauded this vision of kimchi and BTS domination, albeit in more vague words, proclaiming the “fusion of culture with industry” in their inaugural addresses, and prioritizing a “creative economy” that included promoting overseas expansion of talent.4 By the 2020s, Kpop stars were being appointed as presidential envoys for public diplomacy.5

The Korean Wave’s ultimately phenomenal path reveals the markings of explicit governmental direction and organization at every other turn, naturally prompting the question, why? Why would a government still fresh out of its development and industrialization, devote such a large amount of resources to manufacturing, not physical goods, but exporting culture?

Soft Power: Culture as a Geopolitical Tool

The answer is soft power.6 Geopolitical battles are hardly carried out solely by the drone attacks and covert sabotage operations scrolling across BBC headlines. Social media content, animated movies, and catchy tunes all act as ammunition for soft power battles being fought where hard ones cannot. These more obscure struggles occur over a conflict arguably just as significant: the dominance of a particular set of cultural values.7 It has been quipped that Michael Jackson and Coca-Cola kicked the last few bricks out of the Berlin Wall. This contest for culture is, at its roots, defined by each nation’s ability to advertise. From the tactical efforts by the French to propagate their language and culture through the creation of the Alliance Francaise back in 1883, to 19th century propaganda machines in the midst of the split of powers into Allies and Axis, the ability to influence global perceptions of a nation has long been recognized as a crucial piece in the contentious struggle for political hegemony.8 In World War I, for example, Woodrow Wilson’s Committee on Public Information (explicitly created to foster support for the US homefront) was headed not by bureaucratic agents, but by a newspaper man.9 An advertiser.10

Turn to the late 1990s and early 2000s, and Korean music groups like H.O.T. and Sechs Kies had begun pioneering the Kpop formula that would later define its popularity: copying the successes of American music while adding a unique and distinctive Korean twist, e.g. adapting the polished choreography and boy-band structure of NSYNC or Backstreet Boys while layering in Korean-language rap verses.11 12 Imitation is said to be the sincerest form of flattery.

Every nation still evidently needs to find balance between their reliance on soft power and their utilization of hard economic influence or military capacity. South Korea: ranked fifth in global military power, and 13th in nominal GDP, is not lacking in hard power from a global perspective.13 14 From a regional perspective though, this is less impressive. Not one, but three of their immediate neighbours possess nuclear weapons. North Korea, largely recognized as one of the most totalitarian and ruthless regimes in the world, is separated by a meager 4 kilometers Demilitarized Zone. Furthermore, their capacities trail behind those of China and Russia, each tied for second in the Global Firepower Index rankings, and top five in economic rankings along with Japan.15 16 In this environment, South Korea’s endeavor at soft power serves to fill a key gap in basic survival abilities. If their neighbours have bigger toys, it’s only a lessened threat so much as the Koreans have bigger friends.

K-pop’s Global Takeover

In 2025, Korean popular culture has more than simply inserted itself into the international stage, it has quite literally taken over. Writing this, it is nigh-impossible to entirely cover the scale and diversity of the coup. It is certainly no longer a wave. It’s a tsunami.

In the streaming industry, the dominance of Korean media, even despite occasional language barriers, is unmistakable. The Squid Game series, a Korean production, broke new barriers in viewership in 2021, reaching 142 million households worldwide in just a month of release and becoming the most popular Netflix release to date.17 The series single-handedly brought about a rapid expansion in Korean content, which by 2024, accounted for nearly 10% of all viewing hours on Netflix.18 It achieved the point to which high-schooler video game spinoffs of the series on Roblox racked millions of visits. The music industry too: bands like BTS have achieved the title of most streamed male group globally, accumulating over 30 billion streams, with Blackpink crossing a similar barrier as the first girl group with 15 billion. BTS alone reportedly contributes 5 billion USD annually to South Korea’s economy.19

The most recent blockbuster in the world’s spotlight, Kpop Demon Hunters, would take this already unprecedented popularity and go even further. 236 million views on Netflix, the new most popular release of all time.20 Four simultaneous songs in the Top 10, the only soundtrack to do so, with the song “Golden” hitting #1.21 8 straight weeks at the top of Netflix’s English-Language films, and another 7 weeks at #2.22 But all this is not even the intriguing part. What is pivotal about K-pop Demon Hunters is that it is a Korean culture-themed movie, but written and produced almost entirely by Canadians and Americans and the LA-based Sony Pictures Animation.23 The producers wrote songs with distinct K-pop structure, oscillating between Korean and English lyrics with a mix of slow ballads and hybridized rap. Yet, the movie wasn’t even written for Koreans. It was written for a Western audience.

Two decades ago, this would have been unimaginable. Two decades ago, Shrek was topping charts by recycling familiar Western fairy-tale tropes, and Mulan stood almost alone as mainstream Asian representation in children’s films. The success of K-pop Demon Hunters today shows just how thoroughly Western audiences have been primed by a decade of K-pop dominance and sustained South Korean investment in exporting its culture. There is no greater testament to the success of the policies promoting Korean cultural industry than when the very countries whose favor you seek are publishing your content for you, to their own audiences.

The popularity of Kpop culture in recent years is simply unprecedented: from music and movies sung largely in Korean and produced thousands of miles away, to even esports and beauty, K-pop has penetrated the Western cultural market to an astonishing extent, especially among the global youth.24 This dominance prompts a further question: where else do these soft power plays take place?

The Fight Capital of the World

Booming bass tremble through the arena, floodlights and laser beams slice through the dark; a gigantic screen roars to life and flashes the name of the occasion in huge, quivering letters: “UFC FIGHT NIGHT: SAUDI ARABIA”. The throbbing, frenzied crowd bellows their approval as two muscled contestants emerge from smoke-filled tunnels, intense pyrotechnics in full display. Soon, roundhouse kicks follow spinning elbows, takedowns crash into ground-and-pounds, and flying knees meet counter hooks. Most of the crowd, largely action-chasing males, had traveled from abroad, drawn by the spectacle and promise of adrenaline. Typing ‘Saudi Arabia’ into their search bars and booking their Emirates Air tickets, they may have stumbled upon the headlines of damning human rights reports or scandalous state-sponsored murder. They may have had slight misgivings, a small inkling of concern or reservations, but by that blood-pumping moment, the crowd had little on their mind but the event. At the back of their minds, recollections of those condemning article titles grow foggier and foggier. Their image of Saudi Arabia is narrowed down to two things: UFC fights, and maybe oil.

As dissimilar as they might seem, UFC Fight Night and Blackpink music concerts are the same game on different fields: increasingly, it feels as if we are all simply a canvas upon which states can hope to paint their ambitions and goals. In 2024, a large-scale study charting deals by Saudi Arabia in the area of sports stumbled upon a stunning stat: the nation had recently brokered over 900 sponsorships in activities ranging from football to esports.25 Saudi Arabia. The same country that newspapers brand as “ultra-conservative” and label an “exporter of jihadist ideology”, notorious for shaky humanitarian records, is now hosting UFC championships, WWE events, and even Esport World Cups.26

This may begin to feel strange when scrutinized carefully. Why are we watching American entertainment in Saudi Arabia? Do we not literally have our own venues in the middle of the desert, (ahem, Las Vegas)? The truth is, the reason WWE, UFC, or Esports fans are travelling to the Arabic kingdom is because of fundamental political realities: the country is in dire need of a clean, rebranded image; 90% of their exports are oil; their means of diversification rests upon tourism and global investments. These incentives manifest themselves, at the end of the day, in billions of dollars invested in securing and hosting major sporting events.27 For combat-sport fans around the world, all that changed on the surface were slightly more expensive flight tickets. The change that mattered though was the emergence of the conception of an exciting Fight Island. Middle class Americans, for the first time, are excited about a place in the Middle East.

As we consider the broader sporting world, we’ll find that Saudi Arabia’s King Al Saud’s brother owns Manchester United;28 the UAE has waged billion-dollar bidding wars with its Gulf rival for the rights to host events like Formula 1,29 the FIFA Club World Cup, and the Asian Cup; Qatar spent an unprecedented $220 billion to host the 2022 FIFA World Cup;30 and Bahrain has turned its desert into a Formula 1 Grand Prix circuit.31 But the Middle East is hardly alone. China’s Olympics, both in 2008 and 2022, were choreographed to display national strength and modernity;32 Russia’s Sochi Olympics and 2018 World Cup aimed to reclaim legitimacy in the post-Crimea era;33 the United States are exporting their culture with NBA games in Paris, and NFL games in London.34 From Beijing to Riyadh, from Sochi to Seoul, our cherished athletic spectacles have morphed into soft power missiles that are being accumulated by global powers in an escalating arms race.

A Hijacked Culture

If we are the canvas upon which states paint their ambitions, social media is the brush. Social media encompasses much of what we now call popular culture: it’s where lively debates over NFL games unfold, where emerging music stars gain popularity and falling stars literally live stream their fall from space, where trendy and popular expressions, as well as inexplicable memes, are born and circulated. As the tool by which cultural content is framed and dispersed, platforms like Instagram, Tiktok act as a conduit of cultural engineering. Algorithms decide what songs, shows, and images you encounter; they track what you, as well as everyone around you, click, share, dislike, and linger on, and they recommend curated, popular, and artificially-perfect content from there. These forces, along with the dramatic increase in screen time during the pandemic, gave Kpop artists the hyper‑visibility and accessibility they needed. As COVID went viral, so did Kpop, and it wasn’t a coincidence. BLACKPINK’s official YouTube channel, for example, now boasts around 99 million subscribers and over 30 billion total views, making them the most‑viewed group music channel ever on YouTube.35 BTS’s management company even launched its very own dedicated fan-artist media platform in 2019, Weverse, equipped with direct-message channels to popular singers and community discussion features, and over 30 million users by 2025. What we call popular culture is, in practice, whatever social media platforms allow to flourish.

But what is culture at the most fundamental level? We have imagined it as our shared art, language, and rituals; customs that underscore society’s values, embodying the collective expression of the people and providing a source of common ground. In the dawn of mass media, this has meant Disney, Sunday morning gatherings around televisions, and music tastes cultivated from MTV. Tastes became less regional, and more national—a kid in Texas and a kid in Washington might both enjoy Bugs Bunny on their morning weekends. Social media is what has both scaled and individualized these existing dynamics: accelerating popular trends in both speed and intensity. Hence, a generation was ready for Kpop Demon Hunters in a way that their Mulan-viewing parents would never have understood.

In an age where attention is the most valuable commodity, culture intensifies as a mechanism of influence. Entertainment and art are near-hijacked; trends and rising or falling tides of popularity increasingly directed by algorithms maximizing for engagement: algorithms that are manipulatable and able to be taken advantage of, strings that can pull us. What is obscured, intentionally, is who or what is pulling them. To think that pre-teen girls are obsessed with Kpop because it serves the South Korean soft power agenda seems so counter to our notions of childhood that it is difficult to comprehend.

What does it mean for us to enjoy entertainment serving distant entities’ prerogatives? To have our experiences shaped by media operated by algorithms in service to commercial and geopolitical agendas? The idea of culture intertwining with geopolitics isn’t new, even the Romans understood it with their circuses, amphitheaters, and public spectacles. What’s novel today is the pace: Roman circuses couldn’t have been staged in China or Chile overnight.

Childhood and adolescence, already a teeming attraction for corporate persuasions, risks becoming the next battlefront in global dominance and influence. This may have yet to fully manifest, but as the Middle East continues to pour billions of dollars in sportswashing and South Korea continues to show the world the power of exported music and cinema, the arena for soft power will only grow more contentious.

When I grow up, I wonder what I will see on the screen. Will my interest in UFC be proportionate to Saudi Arabian soft power budgets? Will my kids’ next favourite songs be Russian-produced choruses sanding the rough edges from a troubling humanitarian record? Will I know what they watch and why they watch it?

I’m 16 years old, and I wrote this in the middle of my sophomore year, as a purely curiosity-driven response to a phenomenon I had seen. In general though, I’m a guy who’s always loved to read, practically books of any genre, but I’ve only really taken to seriously writing recently. I’ve been finding that it truly is an incredibly illuminating means of thinking—of creating a flow of thought that allows the writer to investigate and understand the world in a way that would have never in a million years hit them through pure introspection. And as a kid growing up amid the liberating/suffocating capabilities of social media, the baffling multi-directional advice related to our future in a world of AI, I would say that there is a lot to think about. There will be more to come…
