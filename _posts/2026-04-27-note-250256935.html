There’s an incredibly consistent feeling of annoyance that writing generated by large language models manages to incite in me. I’ve read and seen a lot of people commenting on this, on Substack for sure, and I think their qualms with “slop” are only the tip of the iceberg.

The change that large language models will create in written language is obvious when we think about it, but the issue is, I really don’t think many people have thought about it.

I’m a high school student, and it’s easy to notice the constant struggle between 1) Be afraid of AI: it’s replacing everything we do, students can’t be exposed to AI, lest they become mindless bodies outsourcing their mind to OpenAI, and 2) Embrace AI: students, teachers, should all be using AI to make themselves more efficient, to become part of the real world and how the real world works.

But there’s a third category: become incredibly lazy, and offload all your work, all actual deep cognitive engagement to Gemini servers. And what’s always insane to me is that a lot of people fall into this category. Not only students, no, but teachers.

The assignment descriptions I am getting in English class — unmistakeably AI generated. In fact, I’m almost sure that as the teacher reads the assignment out loud in class, it’s as much the first time they’re seeing it as the rest of the class. I’ll rant later on why I find this irritating, but nevertheless, the subject is now a recurring joke in the entire class.

There was one instance where the teacher posted an online assignment on “writing an analytical paragraph” on a quote in a book we had been assigned to read. A few of us had read the book, but more had not. Anyhow, after an entire class spent on discussing the quote, analyzing the rhetorical devices used, the imagery used, what the author’s precise intentions must have been, I was a bit suspicious.

“We go north, always north. The land changes as we go—trees get thicker, the air colder and cleaner. We are running, but not away; we’re running toward something. Miig says the north holds our stories, our bones, our dreams. It’s where the marrow remembers. Sometimes, when the fire is low, I think about what it means to be hunted for dreams.”

You’ll notice, none of any of this really means anything. It’s so incredibly stupid when you see this writing. At the end of the class, we inevitably went to look for this writing in the book. When someone pointed out that the quote nowhere to be found on the page that the assignement had cited (and obviously nowhere in the entire book as well), the teacher answered in the most vague way possible, that it must have been assembled from various parts of the book. Well, that defeats the point of it being a quote.

And this is everywhere we look as a students. Creativity, all the benefits of the road to the solution as opposed to a solution by itself, is being eroded at a breathtaking speed. Not only that, it’s almost as if we have model examples as to whether this is ok. Everywhere we look is AI, AI generated slides with bolded writing that, much of the time, don’t mean anything more than the sentence structure itself — “Not…, it’s…” We literally have speakers (only young ones though) that literally talk like AI. And it’s more than just that they sound stupid when doing so, it’s that when we hear them, we know and can envision in our heads that they sat at their computer and asked ChatGPT to generate them an “impressive-sounding” speech. Automatically, they are less respectable.

Now, as I write this, I wonder at how other people are not able to recognize the stupidness of how they sound/how their writing looks. Whatever it is, people have to realize that a lot of students (and people generally accustomed to AI writing) can essentially be thought of as walking AI-detectors. These are the people of the next generation.
