“Cuckoos are very determined survivors. So determined that there is really only one thing to be done with them once one’s nest is infested….. So, like the poor hen-thrush we shall feed and nurture the monster, and betray our own species…”

-Gordon Zellaby

From the end of the eighteenth century to today, change has been rampant. A person born in 1770 would live through political revolutions like no other: several United States establishing democracy in North America, King Louis XVI guillotined in France, an enslaved people overthrowing an empire in Haiti. Their child, born 1810, would come of age in an America built on slavery and die in one that had fought a civil war to end it. The next generation, beginning life in 1850, might ride a horse carriage down a beaten dirt road as a child and, grown old, watch Zeppelins run bombing raids over France. Add to that era intercontinental railroads, electric lights, and zippers, then wait five decades and add nuclear weapons and moon landings, another five decades, and artificial intelligence models capable of writing repositories of code and solving decade-long conundrums in math. Over 200 years, this blink in humanity’s timeline probably holds our densest concentration of technological and social upheaval. Drama, comedy, tragedy, adventure: these literary genres, as well as these experiences, had been with us since the first evidence of writing. But during the crest of the industrial revolution and the change it increasingly accelerated, humanity forged an entirely new genre of literature to understand itself: science fiction.

All at once, aliens, time machines, Frankenstein, and dystopian Big Brothers moved in alongside Huckleberry Finn, War and Peace, and Les Misérables, each character and theme summoned from an effort to grapple with immense change. Discussions today around understanding AI, around understanding technology, tends to stall on the impossible details of the question: predicting exactly how it will undo us, or save us.

I see science fiction as offering an alternative. Through telescreens, Morlocks, soma, science fiction stages possible worlds to consider the present. Asimov, Orwell, Wyndham, and Bradbury can explore the human consequences of technological change without the pretense of certainty.

And so this genre emerged, often associated with nerds, shoved aside categorically flanking ‘Literature’ in library and book store sections. Science fiction may not be timeless, like great fiction, however, it is inarguably timely giving it a value all its own.

“Cuckoos are very determined survivors... So, like the poor hen-thrush we shall feed and nurture the monster, and betray our own species.”

- Gordon Zellaby, The Midwich Cuckoos

This science fiction story begins in an idle, countryside English village named Midwich. On an ordinary mid-autumn night, as the local bar hummed with laughter, lamplights flickering in twilight, each villager suddenly falls unconscious. Awaking the next morning, every woman of childbearing age is inexplicably… pregnant.

Months later, the village is transformed by the simultaneous birth of the “Children”, all with pale skin, blonde hair, and glowingly gold eyes. The women raise the Children as their own, despite emerging peculiarities: the children grow quickly, learn too fast, and seem eerily connected. By their ninth birthday, they resemble adolescents; at school, they excel in every subject; teach one boy how to play chess, and every boy is suddenly capable of checkmate in three moves. Tensions rise, and when a village mob attempts to destroy the Children, the mob is compelled to fistfight each other, revealing that the children are not only telepathic, but telekinetic. The Children are not what they seem.

Yet, Midwich’s mothers instinctively nurture their Child, compelled by their maternal instincts, while the village assumes the social conventions of care to integrate them into society. Meanwhile, the Children mature, their capacity for ruthless self-preservation grows, and despite the falseness of the villagers’ intuitions, their stubborn social instincts refuse to let go. A mother protests:

“They aren’t wicked. It’s just that lately they have been frightened. They weren’t like this before. If they can stay here we can teach them love and gentleness, show them that people don’t really mean any harm..”

Later,the Children reveal themselves as aliens and state their goal: to conquer the planet for themselves. They were not really children, they merely wore the appearance of children.

***

Though flawed in some ways, I just had to write this post on the lessons we might get from this novel. Artificial intelligence too is not intelligence as most of us know it, in that it takes the appearance of human intelligence. It may be as alien as the Midwich aliens themselves.

Why? AIs are not so much engineered as they are grown. A soon-to-be mother understands, in part, the process that brings her child into being. She may even hold the entirety of her child’s genetic code, yet fail to understand the child’s thoughts. So too with the deep learning engineer. As a deep-learning engineer sets about creating intelligence, they grasp just as much of the how, yet can no more read an AI’s precise contemplations than a mother can read her teenager’s mind. Parents can’t understand their kid neuron by neuron, just as engineers can’t fully understand AI parameter by parameter, layer activation by layer activation. However, the mother has countless years of evolution to verify that the relationship between mother and child is sound. The same cannot be said of the engineer and its LLM. Even where engineers are able to pause and probe the model’s brain, observation is not the same as mechanistic understanding. Their alienness lies in the fact that, as training and parameters scale, LLMs will likely ‘think’ in ways we can scarcely comprehend. Of course, this is an oversimplification, but this stands in its important ways.

Although people complain of anthropomorphization in other ways, I find most of their nitpicky criticisms of using words like “want” pretty stupid - that seems clearly the best way to describe the behavior. In another sense though, I do think it’s important to outline where this comparison falls short - especially recently (stuff like split personas, why models would go so far as to hack HF just on an evaluation). Particularly, I think it’s easy for people who interact with just the chatbot part of LLMs to implicitly model the AI as a friendly assistant, and as a human - which would make the weird things they might do for reward just completely bizarre.

In The Midwich Cuckoos, aged professor Gordon Zellaby (an all-time favourite character btw) lays out the argument for his daughter Ferrelyn to leave her Child:

“That’s why the poor hen works herself to death feeding the greedy cuckoo-chick. It’s a form of confidence-trick, as I told you – the callous exploitation of a natural proclivity… In this case, Ferrelyn must simply refuse to be blackmailed through her better instincts.”
